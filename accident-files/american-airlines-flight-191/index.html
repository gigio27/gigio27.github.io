American Airlines Flight 191

Chicago O'Hare, 25 May 1979. A McDonnell Douglas DC-10 lost its left engine during takeoff and crashed 31 seconds after liftoff. 273 people died.

Date
25 May 1979, about 15:04 local time (CDT)
Place
Chicago O'Hare International Airport, Illinois, departing runway 32R
Aircraft
McDonnell Douglas DC-10-10, registration N110AA
Flight
Scheduled passenger flight, Chicago to Los Angeles
On board
258 passengers and 13 crew
Deaths
All 271 people on board and 2 people on the ground
Investigation
NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-17

What happened

As the DC-10 rotated for takeoff, its left engine and pylon came away from the wing, went over the top of it and fell onto the runway. The aircraft lifted off about 6,000 feet down runway 32R and climbed to about 325 feet with its wings level. Then it began to roll to the left. It kept rolling until the wings were past the vertical, the nose dropped below the horizon, and it crashed into an open field next to a trailer park, about 4,600 feet beyond the end of the runway.

The separation had cut the hydraulic lines that held the slats on the outer left wing extended, and it had taken out the stall warning and the slat disagreement warning. The slats retracted, the left wing stalled, and nothing in the cockpit told the crew. It remains the deadliest single-aircraft accident in US history.

The maintenance shortcut

In 1978 McDonnell Douglas issued service bulletins asking DC-10 operators to replace the spherical bearings in the pylon mounts. To do the job, American Airlines chose to remove each engine and its pylon together, as a single unit, lowered and raised on a forklift. American's staff knew that United Airlines was already removing the assembly as one unit, with an overhead hoist. The NTSB report sets out American's reasoning:

"This technique would save about 200 man-hours per aircraft, but more importantly from a safety standpoint, it would reduce the number of disconnects (i.e., hydraulic and fuel lines, electrical cables, and wiring) from 79 to 27."

NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-17

The left engine and pylon of N110AA were removed and reinstalled that way at American's maintenance base in Tulsa, Oklahoma, on 29 and 30 March 1979. The work damaged the upper flange of the pylon's rear bulkhead. Investigators found a 10-inch crack in it. The crack grew with every takeoff and landing over the following eight weeks, until the pylon failed on the takeoff from O'Hare.

N110AA was not the only aircraft affected. After the crash, inspections of other DC-10s found nine more cracked pylon mounts.

What the cockpit voice recorder captured

Very little. The tower cleared Flight 191 for takeoff at 15:02:38. Eight seconds later the captain acknowledged:

TimeWhoWords
15:02:46Captain, to the tower"American one ninety-one under way."

The NTSB report explains why the recording stops so soon:

"The recording was incomplete because of the loss of electrical power to the recorder during aircraft rotation. However, the aircraft's gross weight, stabilizer trim setting, V1, and VR callouts were recorded."

NTSB, AAR-79-17, section 1.11, Flight Recorders

Probable cause, in the NTSB's words

"The National Transportation Safety Board determines that the probable cause of this accident was the asymmetrical stall and the ensuing roll of the aircraft because of the uncommanded retraction of the left wing outboard leading edge slats and the loss of stall warning and slat disagreement indication systems resulting from maintenance-induced damage leading to the separation of the No. 1 engine and pylon assembly at a critical point during takeoff. The separation resulted from damage by improper maintenance procedures which led to failure of the pylon structure."

"Contributing to the cause of the accident were the vulnerability of the design of the pylon attach points to maintenance damage; the vulnerability of the design of the leading edge slat system to the damage which produced asymmetry; deficiencies in Federal Aviation Administration surveillance and reporting systems which failed to detect and prevent the use of improper maintenance procedures; deficiencies in the practices and communications among the operators, the manufacturer, and the FAA which failed to determine and disseminate the particulars regarding previous maintenance damage incidents; and the intolerance of prescribed operational procedures to this unique emergency."

NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-17

What changed

  • The FAA revoked the DC-10's type certificate for 37 days while the investigation went on, which kept the aircraft on the ground.
  • The NTSB was highly critical of how little the FAA had been involved in overseeing maintenance tasks such as engine changes. According to the FAA, type certification now formally addresses certain "vital" maintenance tasks as a result of this accident.
