Space Shuttle Challenger

Kennedy Space Center, 28 January 1986. Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch on mission STS-51-L, killing all seven crew.

Date
28 January 1986
Mission
STS-51-L, the 25th flight of the Space Shuttle programme
Crew
Francis R. Scobee (commander), Michael J. Smith (pilot), Ellison S. Onizuka, Judith A. Resnik, Ronald E. McNair, S. Christa McAuliffe, Gregory B. Jarvis
Breakup
73 seconds into the flight
Investigation
Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident, known as the Rogers Commission (1986)

What happened

From ignition onward, hot gas leaked through a joint between two segments of the right solid rocket booster. The joint was meant to be sealed by two O-rings, and the launch morning was forecast to be the coldest of any shuttle launch. The leak eventually weakened or burned through the external tank, and the vehicle broke apart 73 seconds into the flight.

None of this was a surprise to the engineers. O-ring erosion had been seen on earlier flights for years, and the night before the launch, engineers at Morton Thiokol, the booster's maker, recommended against launching below 53 degrees Fahrenheit. Their management reversed that recommendation.

A known flaw, signed off

  1. 1977Tests measure the joint opening up, or rotating, under simulated launch pressure. The test data show a potentially catastrophic flaw in the booster O-rings, and neither NASA nor Morton Thiokol fixes it.
  2. November 1981First O-ring erosion in flight, on the second shuttle mission, STS-2.
  3. December 1982The O-rings are reclassified Criticality 1: a failure would destroy the vehicle and kill the crew, and the second O-ring no longer counts as a backup for the first.
  4. 1985O-ring erosion on all but one shuttle flight of the year. In July, after a secondary O-ring in a nozzle joint eroded on mission 51-B, showing that the primary seal had failed, a launch constraint is placed on later flights.
  5. Before 51-LThe booster project manager at NASA's Marshall center waives the constraint for six consecutive flights. Senior shuttle managers know neither of the constraint nor of the waivers.
  6. 27 January 1986, eveningA teleconference links Thiokol engineers in Utah with NASA's Marshall and Kennedy centers. Thiokol recommends not launching below 53 degrees Fahrenheit. Thiokol's management then reverses the recommendation, while its engineers keep opposing the launch.
  7. 28 January 1986Launch. Ice has formed on the pad overnight. At 73 seconds, Challenger breaks apart.

Cause, in the Commission's words

"The consensus of the Commission and participating investigative agencies is that the loss of the Space Shuttle Challenger was caused by a failure in the joint between the two lower segments of the right Solid Rocket Motor. The specific failure was the destruction of the seals that are intended to prevent hot gases from leaking through the joint during the propellant burn of the rocket motor. The evidence assembled by the Commission indicates that no other element of the Space Shuttle system contributed to this failure."

Rogers Commission report, Volume 1, Chapter IV: The Cause of the Accident

"The decision to launch the Challenger was flawed. Those who made that decision were unaware of the recent history of problems concerning the O-rings and the joint and were unaware of the initial written recommendation of the contractor advising against the launch at temperatures below 53 degrees Fahrenheit and the continuing opposition of the engineers at Thiokol after the management reversed its position. They did not have a clear understanding of Rockwell's concern that it was not safe to launch because of ice on the pad. If the decisionmakers had known all of the facts, it is highly unlikely that they would have decided to launch 51-L on January 28, 1986."

Rogers Commission report, Volume 1, Chapter V: The Contributing Cause of the Accident

Two estimates of the same risk

In his appendix to the report, the physicist Richard Feynman, a member of the Commission, compared estimates of the chance of losing a shuttle. They ranged from roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 100,000. The higher figures came from working engineers, the very low ones from management. One in 100,000, he pointed out, would mean launching a shuttle every day for 300 years and losing only one. He asked what explained management's "fantastic faith in the machinery".

What changed

  • NASA created an Office of Safety, Reliability and Quality Assurance.
  • Commercial satellites moved off the shuttle and onto expendable rockets.
