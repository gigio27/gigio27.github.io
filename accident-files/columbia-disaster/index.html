Space Shuttle Columbia

Over Texas, 1 February 2003. Columbia broke apart during re-entry at the end of mission STS-107, killing its seven crew. A piece of foam had struck its left wing at launch, sixteen days earlier.

Mission
STS-107, the 113th flight of the shuttle fleet and Columbia's 28th
Launch
16 January 2003, 10:39 a.m. EST, Launch Complex 39-A, Kennedy Space Center
Loss
1 February 2003, about 9:00 a.m. EST, over East Texas, 16 minutes before the scheduled landing at Kennedy
Crew
Rick Husband (commander), William McCool (pilot), Michael Anderson, David Brown, Kalpana Chawla, Laurel Clark, Ilan Ramon (the first Israeli astronaut)
Investigation
Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report, Volume I (August 2003)

What happened

81.7 seconds after launch, at about 65,600 feet and Mach 2.46, a piece of hand-crafted insulating foam broke off the external tank where the orbiter attaches to it. At 81.9 seconds it struck the leading edge of Columbia's left wing. Nobody noticed at the time. Sixteen days later, during re-entry, superheated air entered the wing through the breach, melted its aluminium structure, and the orbiter broke apart.

"The physical cause of the loss of Columbia and its crew was a breach in the Thermal Protection System on the leading edge of the left wing, caused by a piece of insulating foam which separated from the left bipod ramp section of the External Tank at 81.7 seconds after launch, and struck the wing in the vicinity of the lower half of Reinforced Carbon-Carbon panel number 8. During re-entry this breach in the Thermal Protection System allowed superheated air to penetrate through the leading edge insulation and progressively melt the aluminum structure of the left wing, resulting in a weakening of the structure until increasing aerodynamic forces caused loss of control, failure of the wing, and breakup of the Orbiter."

Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Volume I

Foam had struck orbiters before: on 65 of the 79 missions with launch imagery, and bipod ramp foam from the left strut had come off on six earlier flights. None of those strikes had cost an orbiter or a crew, and over time foam shedding came to be treated as an acceptable risk.

The pictures that were never taken

Analysts reviewing the launch video found the strike on the second day of the mission, but none of the cameras had a clear view of where the foam hit. Engineers wanted images of the wing in orbit, which the Department of Defense might have been able to take. Three requests were made, through the wrong channels. One reached the Department of Defense, and NASA cancelled it about ninety minutes later: the chair of the Mission Management Team had called three other members, none of whom knew of a requirement for imagery, and did not consult the engineers who were analysing the strike. Whether pictures would have shown the damage remains unclear.

The investigation board described the engineers' position this way:

"The engineers found themselves in the unusual position of having to prove that the situation was unsafe"

Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Volume I, Chapter 6

That, the board wrote, reversed the usual requirement to prove that a situation is safe. On 23 January, Mission Control e-mailed the commander and the pilot about the strike, relaying that there was "no concern for RCC or tile damage" and, because the phenomenon had been seen before, "absolutely no concern for entry." The board counted eight missed opportunities to find out how badly the wing was damaged.

The rescue the board judged feasible

At the board's request, NASA worked out whether the crew could have come home if the damage had been understood in time. In that scenario a spacewalk on Flight Day 5 finds the damage. Atlantis, already being prepared for a launch on 1 March, could have been ready by 10 February with crews working around the clock. By changing their activity and sleep, Columbia's crew could have kept carbon dioxide at acceptable levels until the morning of 15 February. Suited crew members would have crossed to Atlantis on spacewalks, and Atlantis would have come home with eleven people on board, four on the flight deck and seven on the mid-deck.

"This rescue was considered challenging but feasible. To succeed, it required problem-free processing of Atlantis and a flawless launch countdown."

Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Volume I, Section 6.4

The alternative, repairing the wing in orbit, was rated high risk.

What the board blamed

"The organizational causes of this accident are rooted in the Space Shuttle Program's history and culture, including the original compromises that were required to gain approval for the Shuttle, subsequent years of resource constraints, fluctuating priorities, schedule pressures, mischaracterization of the Shuttle as operational rather than developmental, and lack of an agreed national vision for human space flight."

Columbia Accident Investigation Board, Report Volume I

The board did not single out individual managers. It wrote that NASA's problems could not be solved simply by retirements, resignations or transferring personnel.

What changed

  • The shuttle fleet was grounded for 29 months. Flights resumed in July 2005.
  • Each shuttle then carried a 50-foot inspection boom, used within 24 hours of launch to look for damage, and a rescue orbiter could be launched on short notice.
  • The board recommended that Department of Defense imaging of every flight in orbit become a standard requirement.
