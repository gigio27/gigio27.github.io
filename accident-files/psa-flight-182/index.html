PSA Flight 182

San Diego, 25 September 1978. A Pacific Southwest Airlines Boeing 727 on approach to Lindbergh Field overtook and struck a Cessna 172. 144 people died, 7 of them on the ground.

Date
25 September 1978, 09:01:47 local time (PST)
Place
About 3 nautical miles northeast of Lindbergh Field, San Diego, California. The wreckage fell on the North Park neighbourhood.
Aircraft
Boeing 727-214, Pacific Southwest Airlines Flight 182, and a Cessna 172, N7711G
Deaths
135 on the Boeing, 2 in the Cessna, 7 on the ground
On the ground
9 people injured, 22 dwellings damaged or destroyed
Weather
Clear, visibility 10 miles
Investigation
NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-5

What happened

The Cessna, with a flight instructor and a pilot receiving instrument training, was climbing northeast after a practice approach to Lindbergh Field. The Boeing 727 was flying a visual approach to runway 27. The approach controller pointed the Cessna out, the jet's crew reported it in sight, and the controller cleared them to "maintain visual separation" from it. From then on, keeping clear of the Cessna was the crew's job.

Twenty seconds later the first officer no longer saw it. The crew told the tower they had had it a minute ago and thought it had passed off to their right, and they kept descending. Both aircraft were heading east, the jet descending and overtaking the climbing Cessna. They collided at about 2,600 feet and fell into a residential area.

The alert nobody passed on

At 09:01:28, nineteen seconds before the collision, the conflict alert went off in the San Diego approach control room: the computer predicted that the paths of the jet and the Cessna would come within its warning limits. The jet was by then talking to the tower, and nobody told the tower.

"The approach controller did not inform Lindbergh tower of the conflict alert involving Flight 182 and the Cessna, because he believed Flight 182's flightcrew had the Cessna in sight."

NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-5

The approach coordinator said such alerts were commonplace even when no conflict existed.

Probable cause, in the NTSB's words

"The National Transportation Safety Board determines that the probable cause of the accident was the failure of the flightcrew of Flight 182 to comply with the provisions of a maintain-visual-separation clearance, including the requirement to inform the controller when they no longer had the other aircraft in sight."

"Contributing to the accident were the air traffic control procedures in effect which authorized the controllers to use visual separation procedures to separate two aircraft on potentially conflicting tracks when the capability was available to provide either lateral or vertical radar separation to either aircraft."

NTSB, Aircraft Accident Report AAR-79-5 (1979)

Board member Francis McAdams dissented: he gave the air traffic control procedures more weight. In August 1982 the NTSB amended the probable cause, adopting his view of the failings on both sides, in the air traffic control system and in the cockpit.

What changed

  • On 15 May 1980 the FAA placed the airspace around San Diego under positive radar control, the kind of airspace now called Class B.
  • Lindbergh Field had been the only airport in San Diego County with an instrument landing system. The FAA installed one at Montgomery Field and at McClellan-Palomar, and a localizer at Gillespie Field.
  • In 1981 the FAA decided to implement TCAS, a system that warns pilots of nearby traffic and tells them to climb or descend. After an Aeromexico DC-9 and a Piper collided over Cerritos, California, in August 1986, the FAA required it on large commercial jets in US airspace.
